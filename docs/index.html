Every campaign that works has 4 parts. Most that fail are missing #3.

Here they are:
1) Offer — what you're asking people to do
2) Audience — who you're asking
3) Message-audience fit — the right cause in the right words for that specific person
4) Follow-up — what happens after they respond

Number 3 is where most campaigns quietly die. The cause is good. The offer is real. But the words land wrong because they were written for the wrong person. Save this and check your next campaign against it.

Subject lines that get opened — without clickbait.

Clickbait spikes your open rate once. Then it spends trust you can't buy back. Here are five formulas that work without the tricks:

1) Specific number: "3 families housed this month"
2) Question: "Is your giving page costing you donors?"
3) Curiosity gap: "What we almost didn't send"
4) How-to: "How to write a thank-you donors actually read"
5) Plain benefit: "Your year-end giving receipt is ready"

None of these manipulate. All of them respect the reader. That's the kind of email list worth building.

Your welcome email gets 4x the opens of anything else you'll ever send. Most orgs waste it.

Here's what a good welcome sequence actually does:

1. Deliver what you promised. If someone signed up for a resource, lead with it. No detours.
2. Tell them why you exist. Not your history — your purpose. Why does your work matter to the people you serve?
3. Give one next step. One. Not five links, not a newsletter archive. One clear door.

That's it. Three emails, maybe two. Done well, this sequence turns a stranger into someone who trusts you. Done poorly — or skipped entirely — it's a warm lead you let go cold.

Consistency beats brilliance. A B+ brand applied everywhere outperforms an A+ brand applied nowhere.

Quick audit: pull up your website, your last email, your bulletin, and your social profile side by side. Do they look like the same organization? Same colors, same fonts, same tone?

If someone has to guess whether they belong together, the answer is no.

Faithfulness in small things matters. That principle holds in ministry, in business, and in how you show up visually every single week. The checklist is simple. The discipline is the hard part.
