Before you send the year-end appeal, run it through these 3 questions.

Most appeals fail the reader before they ever get to the ask. They open with the organization's need instead of the donor's role. That's backwards.

Ask yourself:
1) Does it name what actually changed because of giving?
2) Does it put the donor in the story, not your org?
3) Does the ask feel like an invitation or a transaction?

If you can't answer yes to all three, the letter isn't ready. Donors give when they see themselves as the hero of the story. Your job is to show them that role clearly.

Generosity isn't a campaign. It's what happens when people already believe in what you're doing.

Every November, some organizations scramble. They write urgent appeals, set up giving pages, and hope the end of the year does the work that the rest of the year didn't.

Other organizations simply receive. Their donors already know the mission. They've watched it play out in ordinary months, in consistent communication, in work that didn't need a deadline to feel urgent.

The difference isn't the November email. It's what happened in February, May, and August. Trust is built slowly, in the regular work of showing up and telling the truth about what you're doing and why it matters.

Year-end giving is downstream of year-round belief. You can't manufacture that in a campaign.

3 things your donor page should say before it asks for anything.

Most donation pages skip straight to the button. That's the problem. People who are close to giving just need one more reason to believe, and a bare ask doesn't give them one.

Run a quick audit on your page. Does it tell visitors:

1) Who you serve and why it matters
2) What a gift actually does (specifically)
3) Why you, specifically, can be trusted with it

If any of those are missing, the ask lands in a vacuum. Fix the page first. Then ask.
